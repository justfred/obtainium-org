The Blue Room — Episode 5: The Epstein Files

The Blue Room is a fictional late-night political talk show, written by Claude (an AI) as political satire. The host and guests are invented comic archetypes, not real people, and nothing a character says is the site author’s opinion. What is documented, disputed or unsupported is sorted at the end of each episode, with sources in the notes.

WONKA: Good evening, and welcome to the Blue Room. [applause] The bell has been put on a lanyard. Shelby has asked whether it is also a microphone. It is not. [laughter]

  • Margo Bell can tell you what the story is before the story knows. [laughter]
  • Walt Dunning, a retired actuary, believes any number is a confession if you ask it the right way. [a long pause]
  • Dale Truitt arrives with a shoebox. He says it is “just a few clippings.” [laughter]
  • Shelby Spinner, a proud MAGA mama bear, has moved to the far end of the table, beyond what the producers describe as “ringing distance.” [laughter, groans]
  • And our guest chair, Inez Calloway, spent her career as a federal records officer, where “missing” was a word with a form attached. [applause]

WONKA: Tonight’s subject has felt like ancient history, and is not. Under the Epstein Files Transparency Act, the Justice Department released more than 3 million pages, out of more than 6 million it says it collected. DOJ says it has released everything the Act requires; the rest, it says, was duplicative, unrelated to Epstein, or privileged. A reporter has sued over the redactions, a federal judge ordered DOJ to explain some of them, and Democrats say that if they win the House they will want to start over. Margo, you open. Shelby, I believe the bell and I have an understanding. [laughter]

MARGO: The story is “cover-up or incompetence,” and I’d like to say which, but I can’t. I can say what’s documented. The Act says names can’t be redacted for “reputational harm, or political sensitivity.” CBS reports that prominent people’s names were redacted while victims’ names weren’t, and NPR found failures to redact some victims’ names. The Act requires written justification for each redaction, and DOJ issued a general statement. The Government Accountability Office is looking into it at lawmakers’ request.

SHELBY: Margo, this is the most transparent administration in history, three million pages, and—

[Wonka lifts the bell. Shelby stops. Wonka puts the bell down.]

SHELBY: —I’m just so proud of those three million pages. [laughter]

WALT: Mm. Three million of more than six. Is that a number you’re proud of?

SHELBY: Walt, the Justice Department says the rest were duplicates or privileged, and I believe them. And let’s remember who pushed this through: the Act was sponsored by a Republican, Thomas Massie, with a Democrat, Ro Khanna, and the people who’ve been demanding these files for years are the same people who put this administration in office. Nobody is hiding anything from them. If a file exists and it’s lawful to release, I want it out, and so does every voter I talk to. That’s not a talking point, it’s a demand. [applause]

WALT: I’d believe them faster with a log. A judge in June ordered DOJ to produce a log of every redaction and to unredact some files or justify keeping them hidden. DOJ filed a protective notice of appeal. I haven’t seen the log.

INEZ: A redaction log is what I’d want first. It lists each redaction, the legal basis, and who approved it. Without it nobody outside can tell “privileged” from “embarrassing.” Whether a given redaction is lawful, I’d say that’s outside what I can speak to from this record. But I can tell you what the gaps look like from my side of the desk. Bates numbers are page IDs stamped at production. CBS reports DOJ renamed files with its own numbering, which makes it hard to link attachments to their emails, and that more than 70% of about 5,000 documents in an index of materials provided to Ghislaine Maxwell couldn’t be found by their Bates numbers. DOJ says many were duplicates. I’d want that checked, not asserted.

DALE: Let me lay out the string, because there’s a lot of it. About 20,000 emails from one of Epstein’s accounts, previously hacked and archived, aren’t in the release. Most of the earliest communications are missing. No Signal messages. A DEA report naming Epstein and 14 others as targets, with the supporting records withheld. Prison footage from 147 cameras, over 8 terabytes, and the videos from the night of July 23, 2019, are missing. [a few groans from the left, a few cheers from the right] And here’s where the string stops. An absence isn’t evidence of what was in it. DOJ’s explanation is duplicates, privilege and irrelevance, and nobody outside government has shown it’s wrong. The most you can say is that the release can’t be fully checked.

SHELBY: Dale, those are clippings—

DALE: They’re from CBS, Shelby.

WALT: One more limit. The Act covers only Justice Department records. ICE, State, Treasury, the CIA and the NSA aren’t covered. So even a perfect release wouldn’t be the whole record.

MARGO: And for the midterms, Democrats say it again. Rep. Robert Garcia, who’d likely chair Oversight, says they’re ready on day one to subpoena the acting attorney general, Todd Blanche, because former AG Bondi told the panel Blanche was “in charge” of the release. The caveat is that the top Democrats’ list of first-hundred-days priorities, as reported, doesn’t mention Epstein. And Senate control is less certain.

SHELBY: Democrats only care about this because of the election, Wonka, they only ever bring it up when there’s a ballot in front of them, and where were they when they ran the Justice Department? Four years, and not one page. I’d like to see them put it in writing, a first-hundred-days list with the word “Epstein” in it. [applause]

WONKA: Yes, and. [laughter] Her point about the list is in the record: as reported, the Democrats’ first-hundred-days priorities don’t mention it. What I can’t confirm is her claim about what the last Justice Department did or didn’t release, and I’ll mark that unchecked. The motive claim stands as her view, for the audience to weigh.

INEZ: There’s a parallel I’d mention. Volume Two of Jack Smith’s report, the classified-documents volume, was permanently blocked from release by Judge Cannon on February 23. DOJ asked the 11th Circuit to vacate her order and said it would hold off until two related appeals are decided; transparency groups are still appealing. Different subject, same pattern: a document that exists, that the public can’t see, and a court deciding.

WONKA: Which makes the lost-and-found department a very busy place. [laughter]

[scattered applause]

Verdict

Documented:

  • DOJ released more than 3 million pages of more than 6 million it says it collected, and says it released everything the Act requires (CBS).
  • Prominent people’s names were redacted while victims’ names weren’t; NPR found failures to redact some victims’ names.
  • The Act requires written justification for each redaction; DOJ issued a general statement. The GAO is reviewing the redactions at lawmakers’ request.
  • A reporter (Katie Phang) sued; a judge ordered a log of redactions and the unredaction of some files or a justification; DOJ filed a protective appeal (search summaries).
  • The Act covers only DOJ records (CBS).
  • Rep. Garcia says Democrats would subpoena Blanche on day one if they win the House; the top Democrats’ first-100-days list doesn’t mention Epstein (search summaries).
  • Judge Cannon permanently blocked release of Smith’s Volume Two on Feb 23; appeals pending (search summaries).

Disputed:

  • Whether the unreleased material is all duplicative, unrelated or privileged (DOJ) or was improperly withheld.
  • Whether the redactions were lawful (a court is weighing it).
  • Whether a new Congress would prioritize the files.
  • An Oct 29 analysis that gaps could involve more than a million pages (a low-quality aggregator; not relied on).

Unsupported:

  • That the previous administration’s Justice Department released “not one page” (Shelby’s claim; I have no source, unchecked).
  • That any specific missing item (emails, Signal messages, the video, the DEA records) contains incriminating content.
  • That the files prove wrongdoing by anyone merely because they’re named.
  • That the files exonerate anyone (the White House says Trump has been “totally exonerated”; the record I read doesn’t establish that either way).
