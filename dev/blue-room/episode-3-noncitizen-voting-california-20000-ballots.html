The Blue Room — Episode 3: Noncitizen Voting and California’s 20,000 Ballots

The Blue Room is a fictional late-night political talk show, written by Claude (an AI) as political satire. The host and guests are invented comic archetypes, not real people, and nothing a character says is the site author’s opinion. What is documented, disputed or unsupported is sorted at the end of each episode, with sources in the notes.

WONKA: Good evening, and welcome to the Blue Room. I’m told we have a full house and one extra chair, which is the most dangerous ratio in television. [applause]

  • Margo Bell frames the news for a living, and is rumored to have named at least one recession. [laughter]
  • Walt Dunning is a retired actuary who believes a number is only as good as the person who counted it, and has met the person. [laughter]
  • Shelby Spinner, a proud MAGA mama bear, has brought a binder. It has tabs. [applause, a few groans]
  • Dale Truitt spent his career on the overnight dial and has never once been asked what he knows about the daytime. [laughter]
  • Ruth Ellery, tonight’s guest chair, spent thirty years as a county registrar of voters. She has told a great many strangers they were on a list, and a few that they weren’t, and was right about as often as the list was. [a long pause, then applause]

WONKA: Tonight’s subject is a list. On October 15 the Justice Department sued California over about 20,000 mail ballots from people it calls “likely noncitizens.” On October 28 a federal judge denied its emergency request to set those ballots aside, finding DOJ likely lacks standing and a cause of action, and a 9th Circuit panel then rejected DOJ’s bid 2–1. DOJ is appealing. A federal prosecutor says every name was individually reviewed by a federal employee. California says the underlying data is more than a year old. Please hold your questions until the list answers them.

WONKA: Walt, you open tonight. Shelby has promised to wait. She has it written on her hand.

SHELBY: [holding up her palm] It says “wait,” Wonka. And underneath it says “twenty thousand.” [laughter]

WONKA: Walt.

WALT: Mm. A federal prosecutor says every name was individually reviewed by a federal employee. Reviewed how, and against what?

SHELBY: Twenty thousand noncitizens, verified one by one, and the California Democrats are fighting tooth and nail to keep those ballots counted. Every one of them an illegal vote.

RUTH: Every vote cast by a noncitizen would be an illegal one. That’s the law, and I’d enforce it. The question is whether these twenty thousand are noncitizens, and I haven’t seen anyone outside the government say so.

WONKA: Ruth has asked the question the evening turns on. What do we know about how the list was built?

RUTH: Not much that’s been published. DOJ hasn’t explained its method. The reporting says DHS ran California’s public voter list against its own files, from data pulled in August 2025, and says the SAVE database wasn’t used for this list. I can’t speak to anything past that. What I can tell you from thirty years of rolls is that the usual false positive is a naturalized citizen. Someone who became a citizen after the file was made still looks like a noncitizen on an old file. Texas flagged about 100,000 people in 2019, a federal court called it “a solution looking for a problem,” and many of them were naturalized citizens.

DALE: Here’s what I can’t let go. In the summer the government told California it had flagged about 190,000 people. By fall it’s 20,000. Two readings. One: the checking worked and the junk got filtered out. Two: the first list was bad, and the second might be, too. [a few groans, a few cheers] The evidence for the first is a prosecutor’s word. The evidence for the second is Los Angeles County’s sample, where about one in four people on DOJ’s list turned out to be U.S.-born. Where it stops: nobody outside the government has seen the full verification. A judge has ordered the list handed to an immigrant-rights group, and DOJ complied, so for the first time somebody else gets to check the homework.

WONKA: That’s a fair accounting of what’s known and what isn’t, from a man who describes himself as having a map. [laughter]

SHELBY: The Justice Department says—

MARGO: Shelby, can I get one sentence in? [beat] The story the DOJ wants you to take away is “twenty thousand.” The story the judge told is the remedy. Even if every name were right, the judge said DOJ is free to prosecute individual noncitizens who voted, and that setting ballots aside wholesale would “wreak havoc.” I think that’s the answer to the question of why the case keeps losing.

WALT: I’d add the numbers on rarity, because “rare” and “non-problem” aren’t the same claim. Georgia’s Republican secretary of state audited about 8.2 million registrants in 2024 and found 20 noncitizens, 9 of whom had ever voted. Ohio referred 13 voting cases in 2020. That’s rare. It isn’t zero, and Shelby’s right that every one of those is illegal.

SHELBY: Thank you, Walt.

WALT: I’m not finished. DHS later said it found about 2,500 in Georgia. The AJC reported most were pending applicants who never became registered voters, and about 150 had voted at some point, only nine in a Georgia election. The headline and the finding are very different.

RUTH: That’s the pattern I’d watch in any of these. Counting applicants, registrants and voters as the same thing. A noncitizen who registers by mistake at the motor vehicle counter isn’t the same as one who casts a ballot knowing it’s illegal. Federal law has a “knowing” element for a reason.

SHELBY: And a September AJC piece found four hundred thirty potential noncitizens who voted at some point in thirty years, and California’s own motor-voter program sent nearly twenty-three thousand erroneous registrations, and they want us to—

WONKA: The first is real: it drew on SAVE and a state audit, and the same coverage notes SAVE has misflagged citizens. [a pause] “Potential,” “some point,” “thirty years.” Three hedges in one sentence, and I admire them. [laughter] The second is a headline I have in the record but haven’t read, so I’ll mark it unread. Photo ID, though, you’re on firmer ground: Pew found 83% of Americans favor it, 95% of Republicans and 71% of Democrats. That isn’t a partisan position.

SHELBY: Eighty-three percent! [applause]

MARGO: And I’ll say what I think the audience is feeling. The same poll that backs ID isn’t a poll on whether to set aside twenty thousand ballots from a list nobody has checked.

[scattered applause]

Verdict

Documented:

  • DOJ sued California Oct 15 over about 20,000 ballots from people it calls “likely noncitizens.”
  • Judge Carter denied the emergency request Oct 28 (likely no standing or cause of action; the federal crime lets DOJ prosecute individuals but not override California’s ballot processing). A 9th Circuit panel then rejected DOJ 2–1, and DOJ is appealing.
  • DOJ has not published how the list was built; the state says the underlying data is more than a year old.
  • LA County found about 1 in 4 on a sample of the list were U.S.-born (Democracy Docket; D-aligned).
  • Georgia’s 2024 audit: 20 noncitizens of about 8.2 million registrants, 9 of whom had voted.
  • Pew: 83% favor photo ID (95% of Republicans, 71% of Democrats).

Disputed:

  • Whether all 20,000 are noncitizens (a federal prosecutor says each was reviewed; no independent verification published).
  • Why the number fell from about 190,000 to 20,000 (filtering vs. a bad first list).
  • How many “noncitizen voters” DHS found in Georgia (about 2,500 flagged; per the AJC about 150 had voted at some point and only nine in a Georgia election). A September AJC follow-up reports about 430 potential noncitizens who voted at some point in three decades, from SAVE and a state audit; SAVE has misflagged citizens.

Unsupported:

  • That every one of the 20,000 voted illegally (the list is of ballots mailed, not of confirmed noncitizen votes, and no verification has been published).
  • That the number of noncitizen votes has changed any known federal race.
  • California’s “nearly 23,000 erroneous motor-voter registrations” (a headline, not read, so I can’t say what it counts).

Audience reactions

Three people from the audience, asked on the way out. Invented and representative.

  • A woman who became a citizen in 2019, row 2: “I’d hate to find myself on a list like that. I’d also want to know how it was made before I decided who to be angry at.”
  • A man in a ballcap, near the back: “I want ID. I want the list to be right too. Seems like both of those can be true.”
  • A grad student, on the aisle: “The part I keep thinking about is that somebody finally gets to check the homework.”
