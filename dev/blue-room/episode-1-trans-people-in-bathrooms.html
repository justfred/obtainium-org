The Blue Room — Episode 1: Trans People in Bathrooms

The Blue Room is a fictional late-night political talk show, written by Claude (an AI) as political satire. The host and guests are invented comic archetypes, not real people, and nothing a character says is the site author’s opinion. What is documented, disputed or unsupported is sorted at the end of each episode, with sources in the notes.

WONKA: Welcome to the Blue Room. Before we start, my guests. [applause]

  • Walt Dunning is a retired actuary and a lifelong copy editor, which means he has priced every risk in this building, including me, and has questions about my spelling. [laughter]
  • Dale Truitt spent twenty years on overnight radio, never entirely believed the official explanation, and never entirely disbelieved it; he is usually right about what happened and cheerfully wrong about why.
  • Margo Bell can tell you tomorrow’s headline tonight, and which network will run it, and will occasionally write it herself. [laughter]
  • Shelby Spinner, a proud MAGA mama bear, whom I’m told has never once been cut off. She would like that on the record. She will be speaking first. And, I suspect, second. [laughter]

WONKA: Tonight, an easy one. [laughter] I say that every time, and I have never once been right. Tonight’s subject: who may use which restroom, and what the law says about it. The record, briefly. Twenty-one states plus Puerto Rico limit bathroom use by gender identity in some form, per the Movement Advancement Project, an advocacy tracker (October 27). Four make violation a crime in some circumstances. Pew, in February 2025, found 49% of Americans favor sex-at-birth rules and 26% oppose. The Supreme Court has not decided a bathroom case on the merits. Please keep your hands inside the studio.

SHELBY: Wonka, as a mother of daughters, I’d just like to say that women and girls deserve private, safe spaces, and—

WALT: Mm. Safe from what, specifically?

SHELBY: —the American people overwhelmingly understand that men are men and women are women, and a woman should not have to share a restroom with a man. Women. Their spaces. It’s very simple.

MARGO: The story here is a wedge. It’s a manufactured controversy, and in 2026 there are ballot measures in Colorado, Arizona, Nebraska and Washington—

WONKA: Margo, I’m going to stop you there. [beat] “Manufactured” is a claim about motive. Nearly half the country holds Shelby’s view, and I’d guess most of them are sincere.

MARGO: Fair, and forty-nine percent is real. [scattered applause] I’d say the campaigns are strategic. The voters may be sincere. [a few groans from the left side of the studio]

DALE: Can I tell you what everyone’s missing? Loudoun County, Virginia. May 2021, a girl is assaulted in a school restroom, and the district’s answer is to move the boy to another school, where it happens again that October. A grand jury found the superintendent made a false statement at a board meeting. Officials were indicted. Now why would people trust the institutions after that? That’s not a conspiracy. That’s a record.

WONKA: It is a record. The assault was real, the district’s handling was bad, and the grand jury said so. Shelby, would you like to go on?

SHELBY: Thank you, Dale. That case proves exactly why we need these laws. These laws would have prevented—

WALT: Citation? Did the boy use the restroom under a gender-identity policy? And the laws prevent what, exactly, if the assailant was already breaking one?

WALT: The research I’ve seen says that’s not established. The Loudoun case is real, whether a policy caused it isn’t shown, and no state records I know of tie these laws to fewer incidents.

WONKA: Walt has it. The record shows a real assault. It doesn’t show that a trans-inclusive policy caused it. The 2018 Massachusetts study found no change in restroom incidents after protections passed. [a pause] That study was written by people who support such laws, covers one state, and counts police reports, which undercount.

MARGO: And on the other side, the 2022 survey: trans men using women’s restrooms reported about 10% denied access and about 11% verbally harassed in a year. Trans women in men’s rooms, 7% and 9%.

WALT: Self-reports from volunteers, not a random sample.

MARGO: Sure, Walt, but there’s no comparable dataset on the other side at all.

DALE: There’s one gap I’d flag. Nobody’s counting how many cis women get challenged for looking “masculine” under these laws. It’s in commentary and a few stories. I can’t give you a figure, and neither can anyone.

WONKA: Which is the first honest sentence tonight about a number nobody has. [laughter]

SHELBY: The Supreme Court just upheld sex-based rules in sports, six to three, in June, and—

WONKA: Careful. That was sports, a Title IX and equal-protection case, and one education group says it doesn’t reach bathrooms. Another commentator says it opens the door. Nobody has said it settles the question. And Idaho’s law, which reaches many public buildings, was partly blocked by a federal judge on June 16, for six plaintiffs, and is on appeal.

SHELBY: The courts will get it right.

WALT: Which courts?

SHELBY: [smiling] The ones that agree with me.

[laughter, scattered applause]

WONKA: And how, precisely, does the rule get enforced? Texas’s law is complaint-driven, with fines on institutions up to $125,000 a day. Kansas’s requires officials to investigate complaints. Supporters say no one’s being inspected. Opponents worry about exactly that. The statutes are vague on how a rule about sex gets applied to a person standing in a doorway.

WALT: Which is my objection to both sides. We’re arguing about a problem nobody has measured. What would count as the rule working? Fewer incidents, by state, before and after. I haven’t seen those numbers from anyone.

DALE: And this is where trust goes. A rule that runs on a stranger’s complaint gets used on strangers. I’m not saying that’s the plan. I’m saying nobody wrote down who decides.

MARGO: Then here’s my chyron: a problem with no data, and a remedy with no method. [laughter]

Verdict

Documented:

  • 21 states plus PR limit bathroom use (MAP, Oct 27); four with criminal penalties.
  • Pew: 49% favor, 26% oppose (Feb 2025).
  • The Loudoun assaults happened, and a grand jury found the superintendent made a false statement.
  • Trans people report high rates of denial and harassment in restrooms (Williams Institute, 2022 survey; volunteer self-reports).
  • The Massachusetts police-record study found no incident increase after protections passed (one state, advocacy-adjacent, undercounts).
  • The June 2026 Supreme Court ruling is about sports.

Disputed:

  • Whether these laws make anyone safer (no incident data either way).
  • Whether the laws are a pretext for discrimination (the record doesn’t show a uniform motive, and Pew makes “just an excuse” hard to claim for everyone).
  • How far the sports ruling reaches (advocacy sources split).

Unsupported:

  • That any state law or backer intends or requires inspecting anyone’s body.
  • That the Loudoun policy caused the Loudoun assaults.

Audience reactions

Three people from the audience, asked on the way out. Invented and representative.

  • A nurse with two daughters, row 3: “I came in sure it was about safety. I left knowing that nobody has counted anything. That bothered me more than I expected.”
  • A retired electrician, near the back: “A study written by people who like the law doesn’t count for much with me. Same as one written by people who don’t.”
  • A college student: “Shelby talked a lot less than I expected. Walt is the one I’d have a beer with.”

Tag: Wonka, as the lights dim, to nobody in particular: “The door is occupied. It’s been occupied for some time.”
