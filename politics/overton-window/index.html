The Overton Window: Five Rooms, One Hallway

November 2, 2026

The one-line version: the Overton window is the range of ideas the mainstream will treat as sayable and electable right now. It moves. “Radical” means far from the window, and the window sets the distance.

It’s not a ranking of who is right. It’s a map of who is standing where.

Two tests for “radical”

  1. Position. How far from the current center?
  2. Method. Will they work through ballots, courts and persuasion, or break rules, laws or institutions to win?

Method is the better test. A far-off position pursued at the ballot box is unusual. A modest position pursued by breaking things is radical.

The strip

 RADICAL LEFT | LEFT | THE MIDDLE | RIGHT | RADICAL RIGHT
              [========= the window =========]

The window is wide, not a point, and it drifts. Same-sex marriage was outside it in 1995 and sits well inside it now. The edges move when a major party adopts a fringe idea, or when events push the middle.

Room 1: Radical left

Core belief: capitalism and entrenched hierarchy are the root of most harm.

  • Economy: replace or abolish capitalism. Worker ownership, large nationalization, no private landlords.
  • Policing and prisons: abolish or defund; community services instead.
  • Borders: open borders; end most immigration enforcement.
  • Institutions: structurally racist and unreformable, so rebuild the Constitution, the Senate and the courts.
  • Social: reparations; expansive gender-identity policy; some support for restricting speech seen as harmful.
  • Far end: revolutionary Marxism, property destruction, political violence.

Tell: “reform” is an insult. The system is the problem.

Room 2: Left

Core belief: the system works but is rigged toward the wealthy, and government can fix a lot of it.

  • Universal or near-universal health coverage; higher taxes on high earners and corporations.
  • Strong unions, a higher minimum wage, climate action.
  • Abortion rights; LGBTQ protections; police reform plus funding.
  • A legal path for most immigrants; a secure, humane border. ICE is a live split in this room: some want it reformed, some want it abolished (the DNC passed both kinds of resolution in August 2026).
  • Wants the institutions repaired. Does not want them replaced.

Tell: “We just need to pass the bill.”

Room 3: The middle

Core belief: things should mostly work, and the arguing is the problem.

Three kinds live here: the median voter, the cross-pressured (a mix of left and right views, the biggest group), and the disengaged. Few are centrists on everything.

Size: Hidden Tribes (More in Common, 2018) put progressive activists at 8% and devoted conservatives at 6%. The four middle groups, about two-thirds by my arithmetic (the report’s “wings” total 33%), it called the “exhausted majority.”

  • Immigration: secure the border and give long-settled residents a legal path. Rejects open borders and mass deportation.
  • Abortion: legal early, restrictions later.
  • Guns: universal background checks (86% to 96% support in Gallup polls, depending on wording); keeps the Second Amendment; rejects confiscation.
  • Economy: markets work, abuses need rules, the wealthy pay a bit more; Social Security and Medicare are untouchable; cost of living outranks ideology.
  • Policing: supports police and accountability.
  • Elections: voter ID is popular (Gallup: 79% in 2022, 84% in 2024; Democrats about 67% in 2024) and so is easy access; trusts its own vote.
  • Trans issues: broad non-discrimination; leans toward limits on youth sports and youth medical care. Swings with wording.
  • Climate: real; clean energy yes; steep household costs no.
  • Institutions: distrusts Congress, the media and both parties; still believes in the Constitution.

Tell: “Can’t they both just compromise?”

Warning: the middle is a mix, not a midpoint. It can hold positions that look radical in isolation. It is not correct by default. And it follows the agenda that activists and elites set.

Room 4: Right

Core belief: limited government, strong families, and a country that rewards work.

  • Lower taxes and less regulation; school choice; energy production.
  • Border enforcement and lower immigration, with legal pathways kept for some.
  • Abortion restricted, with variation on exceptions.
  • Gun rights; support for police; skepticism of government mandates.
  • Distrusts large federal agencies and parts of the media and universities.
  • Wants the institutions trimmed. Does not want them burned.

Tell: “Government is not the answer.”

Room 5: Radical right

Core belief: the nation is a particular people with a particular culture, and elites are dissolving it.

  • Immigration: mass deportation, ending birthright citizenship, sharply cutting legal immigration. At the fringe, ethnic or religious criteria.
  • Institutions: the bureaucracy, courts, universities and media are captured. Remedies range from mass firings to ignoring court orders.
  • Elections: distrust of the system; support for overturning results.
  • Economy: varies. Some want almost no government. Others want protectionism and welfare for “real” citizens.
  • Social: traditional gender roles; religion shaping civil law; hostility to LGBTQ rights.
  • Far end: white nationalism, accelerationism, militias, political violence.

Tell: “They stole it.”

How the window moves

  1. A fringe idea gets attention.
  2. A major party adopts a softer version.
  3. The middle gets used to it.
  4. The old fringe looks mainstream; a new fringe forms beyond it.

Test case: guns

One issue, five rooms. Same question to each: what should the country do about guns?

Room Position What they’d say
Radical left Ban and buy back most firearms. Treat gun ownership as a public-health hazard. “No other wealthy country lives like this. Ban them, buy them back, and stop apologizing.”
Left Universal background checks, red-flag laws, assault-weapon and magazine limits, safe-storage rules. Keeps hunting and home defense. “Nobody is coming for your deer rifle. We’re asking for a background check.”
The middle Background checks for all sales (86% to 96% support in Gallup polls, depending on wording). Keeps the Second Amendment. Rejects bans and confiscation. Wants something done and doesn’t trust either side to do it. “Check people before they buy. Don’t take my uncle’s shotgun.”
Right Enforce existing laws; focus on criminals and mental health, not owners. Concealed carry; opposes new bans and registries. “We don’t need more laws. We need the ones we have used.”
Radical right No restrictions at all: no background checks, no registries, no licensing. Guns as the last check on government. At the far end: armed resistance to federal agents. “Shall not be infringed. Means what it says. Full stop.”

Where the window sits

  • Inside the window: background checks, red-flag laws (more contested), concealed carry, hunting, safe-storage rules.
  • At the edge: assault-weapon bans (57% support in a 2015 Pew poll; Republican support fell from 54% in 2017 to 37% in 2021; heavily contested), permitless carry, national registries.
  • Outside the window, both ends: mandatory buybacks of most firearms; repeal of all federal gun laws.

Both radical ends sit outside what either major party would run on. The fight in the mainstream is over the middle three rows, and that fight is over the edge items, not the fringes.

What the radicals share

Both fringes distrust the federal government to do this right. One says it won’t act enough. The other says it can’t be trusted with the power. The middle’s version: “Both of you scare me a little.”
