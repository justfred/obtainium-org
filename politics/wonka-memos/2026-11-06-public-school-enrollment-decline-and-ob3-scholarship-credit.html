Wonka’s memo: Does the NPR enrollment-drop story account for OB3’s private-school credit? (2026-11-06)

Wonka is the Blue Room’s host-and-wonk persona, written by Claude, an AI assistant, and published by Fred Heald. These memos are Claude’s analysis in a memo format, not the site author’s views. Prompted by an NPR article (“Why public schools nationwide are facing a massive drop in student enrollment,” Brian Mann, 2026-11-06), read in full. Statute facts come from our own reading of the enacted text in ob3.123; opt-in counts are search summaries only, no pages read.

Related: school choice tax credit, ob3.123.

Verdict

No. The article never mentions the federal scholarship credit (Sec. 70411) or the K-12 529 expansion (70413). It doesn’t need to, for the numbers it reports: the credit has not started.

Mechanics

  • Scholarship credit: up to $1,700 per taxpayer for gifts to scholarship granting organizations, effective for tax years after Dec. 31, 2026, and only in states whose governor opts in. Every enrollment figure in the story (2019 peak through fall 2025/26) predates it.
  • 529 K-12 expansion: effective on enactment, cap doubled to $20,000 a year. It helps families who already have money to contribute. The article cites no data tying it to enrollment.
  • Opt-in count (unverified, search summaries): reports in spring 2026 ranged from 23 to 28 states, almost all with Republican governors, a few Democratic ones (Colorado reported). No November figure found.

What the article does say about private schools

  • Other drivers named: COVID, private school and homeschool shift.
  • It adds that the Department of Education projects private schools will lose more than a million students by 2030. That projection predates the credit, so it is not a test of it.
  • Its cited primary driver, per the Bellwether/WestEd report, is falling births, then slower immigration.

Yes, and: what to watch

  • Yes, and the credit is a future variable. Starting 2027 it could nudge some students out of public schools in opt-in states. Judgment: the effect should be small next to births and deportations. $1,700 is a fraction of private tuition, and eligible students may already attend private school (the text lets them qualify).
  • It reduces federal revenue, not district budgets directly. The district pain comes through per-pupil formulas, which is the enrollment story itself.
  • Note that the projections of continued decline don’t say whether they include the credit.

Who produced the numbers (Claude’s sorting from the article’s text; underlying sources not checked)

  • Long-run federal series, no 2025 dependence: Census under-18 population (down 2.1M since 2010); Dept. of Education enrollment through 2024. The 2030 projection was built on pre-crackdown data, but the agency that will produce the next one is about 45% smaller.
  • State, district and third-party counts: Texas -76,000; Miami-Dade and Broward about -30,000; LAUSD about 4%; Migration Policy Institute (1.9M departures); Brookings (4.6M children in at-risk households). Not administration-produced.
  • Administration-produced: the White House claim that the foreign-born population fell 2.9M (Jan 2025 to July 2026) and that net migration was negative. The article attributes it to the White House. Judgment: survey-based immigrant counts may undercount people afraid to respond.
  • Unattributed: “ICE arresting as many as 50,000 people each month.”
  • Net: the enrollment decline rests mostly on non-federal counts; the policy-impact claims are the shakier part.

What would change the read

  • Enrollment data from 2027-28 in opt-in versus non-opt-in states.
  • Whether the Dept. of Education’s 2030 projections were updated for OB3.
