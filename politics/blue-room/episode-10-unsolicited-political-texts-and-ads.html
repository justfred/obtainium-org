The Blue Room — Episode 10: Reply STOP

Wonka Good evening, and welcome to the Blue Room. Tonight’s subject is the unsolicited political text, and its louder cousin, the political ad. Nobody on this panel gave anyone permission, and the line outside is, I’m told, a little longer than expected. [laughter] Tonight we start with Dale, because he’s been holding his phone out like a dowsing rod since the break.

  • Dale Truitt, twenty years of overnight radio, has received eleven texts since we sat down and would like to know who told them where he was. [laughter]
  • Margo Bell will walk us through the life of a text, step by step, and has promised not to enjoy it. [laughter]
  • Walt Dunning, retired actuary, has read the terms of service of his own phone plan, twice, with a pencil. [laughter]
  • Shelby Spinner, a red-hat regular, says her number is on every list in the country and she’d like to know who put it there. She has been asked to wait for her turn, and has written “WAIT” on her palm, in a smaller font than last time. [laughter]
  • And our guest chair, Tess Okafor, spent a decade as compliance counsel for campaigns of both parties. She’ll tell us only what the record supports, and she’s already looked at the exit. [applause]

Wonka Five slides. The page we read in full is at the bottom; the rest are search summaries.

1. The list. Campaigns on both sides build from the voter file: public registration and vote history, plus phone numbers added by data vendors. [1]

2. The exemption. Political calls and texts are exempt from the Do Not Call registry. Autodialed texts need consent; hand-keyed ones don’t. [2]

3. The STOP. “STOP” removes you from that sender’s list. Not the voter file, not the next group. [1]

4. The volume. About 15 billion political texts in 2022, at as little as a cent each. No 2026 total found. [3]

5. The ads. A record $11.2 to $11.9 billion in political ad spending this cycle, depending on who’s counting and when. [4]

Wonka Fifteen billion texts in 2024, and—

Walt 2022. Mm. Citation? [laughter]

Wonka I rounded toward the future. [laughter]

Dale Let me say what I found, because the string led somewhere real this time, and I’m a little shaken. I expected a secret database. It’s the county elections office. In many states a campaign can get the voter file free or cheap, and then a vendor attaches your cell number from everywhere else you’ve ever typed it. Petitions, donation forms, RSVPs. The conspiracy is a public record request and a shopping cart. [laughter] I’d like it noted that I’m disappointed.

Margo Let me walk you through it. Step one: you register, and the state hands your name, address and vote history to anyone who asks. Step two: a vendor matches you to a phone. Step three: a campaign rents the list, because texting costs about a cent and a TV spot costs a car. Step four: it’s peer-to-peer, a human taps “send” on a script, so it counts as hand-keyed. Step five: you reply STOP, and a different committee rents the same list. [laughter] And my side does this too. The fundraising spam from Democrats has its own critics, including on the left, and I’d like them to win. [scattered applause]

Wonka Yes, and “my side does it too” is the right sentence, and not often said first. [laughter]

Tess I can speak to the mechanics. The rule turns on how the message was sent. An autodialed text needs prior consent. A message a person keys in, or sends one at a time, doesn’t. Platforms build the product around that line. As for how often anyone’s been penalized, one strategist in the piece we read doubted anyone ever was; that’s one person’s opinion, and I can’t speak beyond it. And STOP is per sender: the sender honors it, the next sender has never heard of you.

Walt I have a denominator. Fifteen billion texts, 2022. Call it about two hundred sixty million adults, which is my number, not the record’s. Roughly fifty-some texts a year each, and presumably it isn’t evenly spread. [laughter] What I can’t find is how many people were reached versus how many were sent the same seven messages. A text is a count. A person is a different count.

Margo And there’s the part nobody warns you about. Chip in once, on any donation site, and the volume goes up. Donors say it’s a flood, dozens a day after one gift. [groans] And the sites say they don’t sell your number.

Walt Both can be true, and I’d like that noted. What would we see if the site were the leak? What we see now: texts. What would we see if the campaigns were sharing or renting lists? The same texts. The reports are from donors and forums. I found no audit that tells the two apart.

Tess I can only speak to what the help pages say: the donation platform says it shares your phone and email only with the campaign or group you gave to, and the phone number is optional. What a campaign then does with its own list is outside what I can speak to.

Dale Which is the dot. The donor list is the most valuable list a campaign owns, because you’ve already said yes once. I’ll hold it loosely, but that’s where I’d put the string.

Margo I’ll be fair to the other side of the aisle: it’s a bipartisan complaint. Republican donors describe the same thing, and the national party was sued over constant fundraising texts. [scattered applause]

Shelby As a mom, I’d like my turn. [laughter] My phone is a campaign-finance hearing. My side sends them, I know it does, and I’ve asked them to stop, and I’ve gotten four more from a group I’ve never heard of with a name that changes every week. And I don’t love saying this: when it’s my side, I’m the one who has to see the fine print. I’m not for banning political speech. But “you have to opt out one sender at a time, forever” isn’t a system. It’s a [bell]

Wonka Once per episode. You get the full turn in a minute, Shelby. [laughter]

Dale Can I be kind? The fake ones scare me more than the real ones. A report counted over five thousand malicious midterm-themed web addresses in a month, some spoofing the donation sites, with lures like “200% match.” The secondary write-ups are all I have, so I’ll hold the number loosely, but it’s the dot I can’t un-see. A real text and a scam text look the same until you hit the link.

Tess The legitimate donation pages in the write-ups sit on the processors’ own secure domains. Other processors exist, so the address alone isn’t proof. That’s as far as I can go.

Walt So how does a person tell the real text from the fake one? Mm. Citation?

Tess What the consumer-security and news pieces we found advise, and these are advice pieces, not rules: check the address on the payment page, because the two big platforms’ pages sit on their own “secure” addresses; look for a “Paid for by” line; treat urgency, a “200% match” for the next ten minutes, as a warning; and don’t give money through the text at all. Go to the candidate’s own site yourself. Better grammar no longer proves anything, because AI has fixed the typos. [8]

Dale So the safest dot is the one you draw yourself. [laughter]

Wonka Yes, and a listener has a proposal for the fix. Every political call and text must come from one number per organization, and grassroots volunteers must route theirs through the organization’s number. [a long pause]

Margo I like it, and I can already hear the volunteers. A neighborhood group would have to go through the party’s number to say hello. [laughter] But it would make a “STOP” mean something.

Walt What would we see if it worked? One number to block, one number to complain about. What would we see if it didn’t? A lot of new organizations. The record I have says STOP works per sender; it doesn’t say what counts as a sender. That’s the whole question.

Tess That’s a design question, and it’s outside what I can speak to. I can only say the line between “sender” and “volunteer” is exactly where today’s loophole is.

Shelby I’ll say this one is fair to everyone, and I’d say it if it cost my side. [applause]

Margo And the ads are the polite relatives. $11-point-something billion, mostly broadcast, with connected TV and digital growing. The difference is you can change the channel. You can’t change your number. [laughter]

Walt I’d test that. What would we see if the ads were harmless? Nothing different. What would we see if they weren’t? Nothing the search turned up. The spending is a fact. The effect on a voter isn’t in front of me.

Shelby Thank you. And can I finish? I’m not for banning speech. A small campaign with no money can reach a neighborhood for a cent a text, and that’s the same speech as a billionaire’s. But a rule that covers everybody, not just politicians, is a different thing. If a pizza place needs your consent, so should I. [applause]

Margo That’s the part I’ll take from her. Same rule for everyone, which would be fair, and I’d be annoyed that the campaigns I like lose a tool.

Wonka Yes, and “a rule that applies to every sender” is a proposal, not a finding. Whether it would survive a court isn’t in our record. New rule: a candidate may not text “this is the last one.” [laughter] Reply STOP. [a long pause]

Verdict

Documented:

  • Consumer-security and news advice for spotting a fake donation text: check the payment address (the big platforms use their own “secure” addresses), look for “Paid for by,” distrust urgency and “match” lures, and donate through the candidate’s own site; forwarding a text to 7726 is a reported way to report it. These are advice pieces, not official FEC or FTC guidance. [8][9]
  • Campaigns of both parties build from a voter file (public registration and vote history) enriched by vendors with phone numbers. [1]
  • Political campaign calls and texts are exempt from the National Do Not Call registry; autodialed texts need prior express consent; manually sent texts do not. [2]
  • “STOP” removes you from that sender’s list only, not the voter file or other senders. [1]
  • The FCC ruled on 2024-02-08 that AI-generated voices are “artificial” under the TCPA and need consent. [5]
  • About 15 billion political texts were sent in 2022 (one tracker’s figure), and political ad spending this cycle is a record, $11.2 to $11.9 billion depending on source and date. [3][4]

Disputed:

  • Whether a one-number-per-organization rule (a listener’s proposal, not a finding) would work. No source tested it; the open question is who counts as a “sender.”
  • Whether campaigns have ever been penalized for mass-sending without consent. One strategist doubts it; no enforcement count was found. [1]
  • How far the scam angle goes. Counts of fake midterm sites and leaked donation-site logins are from secondary write-ups, not the primary report. [6]
  • That donating sharply increases the texts and emails you get. Many donors report it (forum and Daily Kos posts); one report cites up to 60 a day. The donation platform says it shares your phone and email only with the campaign you gave to (phone number optional) and does not send messages for other groups. The Republican platform, WinRed, says the same: messages come from campaigns and groups, and it doesn’t rent or sell donor data; Republican donors report the same flood, and the RNC was sued over WinRed fundraising texts (one complaint, not a ruling). Whether the extra volume comes from the campaigns’ own lists, rentals or swaps is not established; no audit was found. [8]
  • Whether peer-to-peer texting is a loophole or simply the legal line. The platforms’ own blogs say it’s a gray area; those are vendor sources.

Unsupported:

  • A 2026 total for political texts. None was found.
  • That voting reliably gets you off the lists. The source says it takes “a day or two” after the county’s voted list is processed; one source, and not a guarantee.
  • That a rule covering all senders would survive a First Amendment challenge. Not researched beyond a memory-only reference to a 2020 Supreme Court case, unverified.
