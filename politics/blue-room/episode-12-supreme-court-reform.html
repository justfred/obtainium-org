The Blue Room — Episode 12: Nine Is Just a Number

Wonka Good evening, and welcome to the Blue Room. Some housekeeping first. As you heard last time, Shelby Spinner is taking time off to spend with her kids, who have, I’m told, been very patient. Her binder is in the lost-and-found, the bell is back in the prop department, and the door, as ever, is occupied. [a long pause, scattered applause] Tonight’s subject is how to fix the Supreme Court, the one institution everybody wants reformed, in the direction that happens to suit them. A post has been making the rounds with six ideas. We’ll start with our newest regular, because it’s rude to make a guest wait. [laughter]

  • Anji Perera ran a Florida county party and a statewide field operation, and now has a podcast with more downloads than she’ll admit. She has knocked on more doors than anyone on this stage has opened. [laughter]
  • Walt Dunning, retired actuary, is here to disagree with whatever you were about to say, and has confirmed that this includes you. [laughter]
  • Margo Bell, who has described the Court as “a mood” on four different networks, will explain how a reform gets sold. [laughter]
  • Dale Truitt has a theory about unsigned orders and has been telling the stage crew to be careful what they sign. [laughter]
  • And our guest chair, Marian Pell, spent six years as a law clerk on a federal appeals court, where she learned that the most dangerous document in the building is the one nobody wrote a reason for. She’ll tell us only what the record supports. [applause]

Wonka Five slides. One page was read in full; the rest are search summaries.

1. Term limits. The standard proposal: 18 years, one appointment every two years. Polls run about 65 to 75 percent in favor. [1]

2. Size. The Court has had five to ten members in its first 80 years; the Judiciary Act of 1869 set nine. [2]

3. Ethics. The Court adopted a code in 2023 with no enforcement mechanism. A bill to bind it passed a Senate committee on a reported 11 to 10 party-line vote. [3]

4. The emergency docket. One report counted 63 emergency decisions against 56 merits decisions last term. Another tracker counted 32 on administration actions since January 2025. [4]

5. Elections and enforcement. Thirty-eight states make their own state supreme court justices face voters in some way. Federal justices never do: the President nominates and the Senate confirms. The US Marshals sit inside the Justice Department. [5]

Wonka Nine justices, set in 1866 by the nine circuits, and—

Walt 1869. Mm. Citation? [laughter]

Wonka The post said 1866. I trusted it. The 1869 act set nine; the post’s year is the error. [laughter] Anji, welcome. The floor is yours.

Anji Thank you, and may I? [laughter] Let me tell you what the neighbors say: nobody in my county can name three justices, and everybody has a strong opinion about all nine. So I’ll start where the polling starts. Eighteen-year terms are popular because they’re simple, and I think my side should say so instead of saying “never.” The part I’d rather not say out loud is that conservatives like the Court as it is right now, and that is not an argument. If the rule is good, it’s good when the other party’s picking.

Walt I’ll disagree with the room, which is my job. Popular is a poor reason to amend anything. The arithmetic is tidy: two seats a term, four over two terms, never five. But I can’t find the transition, who gets the first seats, or the bill text on a death or an early retirement. Until then “no president could appoint a majority” goes in the second column, and I’d note that the people most pleased with the tidy version have not read the untidy part. [laughter]

Margo And I’ll walk you through why it sells. Two-thirds, three-quarters in favor. When was the last time two-thirds of Americans agreed on a sandwich? [laughter] The story is that everyone wants it, so the only fight is how. And I’ll needle my own side: expansion backers call it restoring balance, and balance has a way of meaning “the seats we’d get.” [scattered applause]

Marian I can speak to the legal split, from the sources. Article III says judges hold office “during good Behaviour.” One camp says a statute can’t end that; the other says justices could move to a senior role after eighteen years, as lower-court judges do. Biden’s own commission said it didn’t know. Which camp is right is outside what I can speak to.

Dale I’d say the interesting dot is that nobody in the room can say. A reform that might be unconstitutional, decided by the institution being reformed. [laughter] And then there’s the emergency docket, where orders that bind the whole country sometimes arrive with no names and no reasons. I’m not saying anyone’s hiding anything. I’m saying “unsigned” is the one conspiracy that’s published. [laughter]

Walt Denominator. Sixty-three emergency decisions against fifty-six merits, one report. Thirty-two administration emergency cases, another tracker. Different counts of different things. Most emergency orders are routine; the fight is over a small number with national effect. Count the sets before you pick a number.

Marian From the clerk’s side: emergency means no time for full briefing. That’s the defense, and it’s fair for a stay. It’s weaker for an order that changes the law everywhere. That’s a general point, not a count.

Anji Here’s where I’d agree with Margo, which my group chat will hear about. [laughter] Say why. A paragraph. If a ruling binds the country, a voter in my county should be able to read the reason without a law degree. That isn’t a revolution. That’s homework. And I’d add that four new seats isn’t homework: it’s an invitation for the other side to add four back, and then somebody’s explaining to a ninth-grader why the number is seventeen. [applause]

Margo I’ll take that, with one amendment: the reasons shouldn’t depend on who’s winning.

Margo Here’s the version I’d actually sell. Thirteen seats, to match the thirteen federal courts of appeals, phased in slowly, one seat at a time over several terms, so no president gets a surge. Each justice could cover one circuit. [scattered applause]

Walt Devil’s advocate: thirteen is an odd number of reasons, and “match the circuits” is a rationale, not a rule. The circuits can change too. Congress set nine by statute in 1869 and could set any number. The question is what stops the next Congress from adding four more. A slow phase-in is only as slow as the next majority wants it to be.

Marian I can say the number of seats is Congress’s to set, and has been since 1869. On the circuits, the sources I saw say there are thirteen courts of appeals, and that under a federal statute each justice is allotted to one or more circuits by order of the Court, handling certain matters like emergency applications from there. I don’t have today’s assignments. Whether a phase-in could bind a future Congress is outside what I can speak to.

Anji Honestly, the slow phase-in is the part I’d listen to. It’s a promise about restraint, and the only way I trust restraint is if both parties have to sign it before they know who benefits. [applause]

Walt On electing them. I should say which courts. Federal justices are nominated and confirmed, never elected. It’s thirty-eight states that make their own supreme court justices face voters, and the money is the story there: about $144.5 million in one 2025 Wisconsin race. The research I saw says the problem is partisan contested elections plus big money, and that retention votes look milder. I’d note that appointment has its own politics. Ask the Senate. And as a lifelong independent who has voted for both parties and regretted each in turn, I’d note nobody’s called for electing the federal ones. [laughter]

Wonka Regretted each in turn?

Walt On a rotating schedule. I keep a spreadsheet. [laughter, scattered applause]

Dale For federal justices it would take an amendment. I found no serious sponsor. So I’ll say the quiet part: this one is a dare. [laughter]

Marian Same for the Marshals. They’re part of the Justice Department. A judge who finds the executive in contempt depends on the executive, or on a private prosecutor, a workaround whose legality is untested. I found no bill that moves the Marshals.

Anji And that’s the one I’d want fixed by both parties together, because both will hold that office someday. It’s the same chair, different year. [applause]

Wonka New rule: a reform must be proposed while nobody knows who benefits. [laughter] We’ll need to find that day. [a long pause]

Wonka Five seats, one golden ticket, and it was reassigned without a written reason. [laughter] Good night.

Verdict

Documented:

  • 18-year terms with an appointment every two years is the standard proposal (Biden, July 2024); polling runs about 65 to 75 percent in favor, depending on wording. [1]
  • Article III guarantees tenure “during good Behaviour”; scholars split on whether Congress can set terms by statute. [1]
  • The Judiciary Act of 1869 set the Court at nine, matching the nine circuits set in 1866 (Federal Judicial Center, read). The post’s “set at 9 in 1866” is wrong. [2]
  • The Court adopted a code of conduct on Nov 13, 2023; per CRS it is not a basis for liability and has no enforcement mechanism of its own. [3]
  • The US Marshals Service is part of the Justice Department (background knowledge, not searched). [5]

Disputed:

  • Whether a statute can impose terms or only an amendment can. Scholars are split; no court has ruled. [1]
  • Whether “no president could appoint a majority” holds through the transition and for early vacancies. True in steady state; bill text on transition was not found. [1]
  • How common unexplained emergency orders are. Counts differ by what is counted: 63 vs 56 (one report); 32 administration decisions (another tracker). [4]
  • Whether campaigning makes judges less fair. Studies find a link in partisan-election states and not in nonpartisan ones; other scholars say causation is hard to show. [5]

Unsupported:

  • The post’s counterfactuals (what different terms would have produced in Citizens United, Shelby County or Roe). Nobody knows who would have been appointed.
  • That “13 justices means three appointments per term” appears in any bill found; it is Wonka’s arithmetic.
  • That a bill to move the Marshals under the judiciary exists. None was found.
  • Any serious proposal to elect federal justices. None was found.
  • That one justice per circuit would work at thirteen seats. The thirteen circuits and the statutory allotment of justices to circuits (28 U.S.C. § 42) are in search summaries, but today’s assignments were not found. Margo’s phase-in idea is her argument, not a finding.
