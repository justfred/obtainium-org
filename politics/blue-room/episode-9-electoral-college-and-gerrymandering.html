The Blue Room — Episode 9: Taken for Granted

Wonka Good evening, and welcome to the Blue Room. Tonight’s subject is the Electoral College and the district map, which we’ve lumped together because they share a flaw: they both decide who wins, and people say they’re less interested in who’s served. We’ll be asking tonight what it’s like to live in a state that’s already been called. [laughter]

  • Margo Bell, who has been sold to more “safe” states than a used-car lot, would like to discuss being taken for granted. She’ll go first if she can find the pencil. [laughter]
  • Walt Dunning, the retired actuary, wants to know what we’d expect to see if “taken for granted” were false. He has brought a ruler. [laughter]
  • Shelby Spinner, a Truth Social regular and, she’d like it noted, still a mom, says she knows exactly what it feels like to be ignored by a state’s politicians, and she’ll tell us which one. [laughter, a few groans]
  • Dale Truitt has a flight tracker open on his phone and has not said why. [laughter]
  • And our guest chair, Gil Marchetti, spent nineteen years as a staff map-drawer for a state legislature, and has watched an office full of constituent files get put through a shredder because a line moved. He’ll tell us only what the record supports. [applause]

Wonka A few slides. Sourcing is at the bottom, and all of it is this morning’s search summaries.

1. The visits. One tally counted 201 campaign visits by both 2024 tickets to the seven swing states. [1]

2. The money trip. A Fox report said Trump expected about $27.5 million from three California fundraisers and one in Las Vegas; Harris held sold-out Bay Area fundraisers. A political scientist called fundraising stops, not campaign stops, a normal part of every race in California. [2]

3. The safe seats. Cook’s Oct 23 House ratings had 186 Solid D and 168 Solid R, about 354 of 434 rated seats. Of 35 Senate races, our own list had 15 as safe: seven R, eight D. [3]

4. The churn. Prop 50 redrew five California districts. Four incumbents are running in a different district than the one they held. District 41 was Ken Calvert’s for more than 30 years. [4]

5. The compact. Nineteen jurisdictions have joined the popular-vote compact, 222 electoral votes, 48 short. [5]

Wonka Five slides, five hundred and thirty-five electors, and—

Walt Five hundred thirty-eight. Five thirty-five is Congress. Mm. Citation? [laughter]

Wonka I counted the ones who go home. [laughter]

Margo Let me walk you through being a blue Californian. Step one: you’re the biggest state, fifty-four electoral votes by itself. Step two: the result is never in doubt. Step three: so nobody comes except to ask you for money. Step four: you’re a block. Not a voter. A block. [applause] Seven states got the visits and California got the invoices. And the fundraiser is the nicest part: it’s a visit that isn’t to you. Step five: nobody has to promise us anything. We’re already in the bag. [laughter]

Walt I’d like to test that. What would we see if it were false? We’d see candidates holding public events in safe states, and making promises there. The visit tally says the seven swing states, and we have a report about money from California. That’s consistent for the visits. The promises I can’t test: I found no study of what candidates offer by state. “Ignored” is an experience. I’d want the measure.

Margo The measure is in my mailbox. Two hours of attack ads and none of them from anybody who knows the name of my street. [laughter]

Wonka Yes, and that’s a fair point about attention. The record shows where the visits went. It doesn’t show what the visits would have done.

Shelby I’d like a turn, as a mom. [laughter] You think California’s alone? I know what it’s like to be a conservative in a deep-red county that’s already called. Nobody visits Alabama either, and nobody asks a Republican in Los Angeles what she wants. A safe state is a safe state. The same thing happens to a red voter in a blue state: you’re in the minority, nobody comes, and you’re a footnote in your own gov [bell]

Wonka Once per episode. She gets a full turn in a minute.

Dale I’ll say what I’ve been looking at, which is the flight tracker. Fundraising stops go in and out of California in a day. I’ve been told that’s normal. I’m keeping an open mind about whether it’s a pattern or a coincidence, but I’ll say it’s an odd form of respect, to land, take the check and leave before dinner. The string runs out at the airport. [laughter]

Gil I can speak to the map side. When a district moves, the incumbent’s staff don’t just get new addresses. The caseload, the relationships with the city and county staff, the list of who’s been waiting on a passport or a veterans’ claim: all of that is tied to a place. When the line changes, a member either keeps working a district half of which isn’t theirs anymore, or starts over. The reports say four incumbents are running in different districts this year. I can’t tell you what happened to anyone’s casework. That’s outside what I can speak to.

Margo So the person who represents you changes, and they’re thinking about the next election more than the last one. That’s the process eating the service.

Walt I have a denominator problem. The search didn’t find how many Californians got moved, and I can’t find a study of what happens to constituent service when a map moves. So the claim is plausible and unmeasured. I’d put it in the second column.

Shelby Thank you. And Margo’s right about the promises, and it’s worse than she says, because it works both ways. If a state is a lock for you, you don’t have to promise it anything. If it’s a lock against you, you don’t bother. Either way the voter is the same size and the candidate’s answer is “next.” [applause]

Margo That’s the part I’ll take from you. The red voter in my state and I have the same complaint.

Walt And last night makes it worse, in a quiet way. The ratings had about 354 of 434 House seats as solid for one party. Most seats weren’t in play, and nobody talked about them. This show spent a week on Alaska. I’d like that noted. [laughter]

Wonka Yes, and “most of the map is decided before anyone votes” is documented. The coverage point is yours: we cover the close ones, because the close ones are the story. That’s a choice, and it’s a reasonable one, and it’s also exactly how a safe seat becomes invisible.

Margo Fine. Then the college isn’t about the money. It’s about the leverage. If you can’t lose us, you can’t owe us.

Wonka I’d put that in the second column, Margo. The logic is sound and the evidence isn’t in the record. Nobody’s measured promises by state.

Gil And the fix. A popular-vote system would change where the visits go. A district-by-district map change would change who’s in the room. Neither is guaranteed to put anyone’s needs first; a competitive seat or state gets promises, and a safe one doesn’t. That’s mechanics. What the voters do with the new attention is a separate question.

Dale I’d like to say something kind. The mailer I’d like is the one addressed to “Our Valued Block.” [laughter, applause]

Wonka New rule: a candidate may not say “every vote matters” in a state that was called at 8:01 p.m. [laughter]

Wonka Before the verdict, an agenda item. Each of you proposes one topic for the next run of episodes. Walt first.

Walt Mississippi. A Senate race that sat at forty-three votes overnight, with a recount rule I haven’t read. What does one vote weigh at that scale?

Margo What “wave” means. NBC says 258 seats. I want to know how much is the year, how much is the map, and how much is the pundits. I’ll do my own side first. [laughter]

Dale Certification, and who can stop it. The DOJ suits, the county boards, the Alaska decoy. I’d like to put the string on the table.

Shelby Why Latino voters moved, and I want a guest who lives there. Not a pundit. [applause]

Gil What a House member’s district office actually does. Casework, who walks in, what gets answered. I think it’s the missing half of every map argument.

Wonka Five topics, one golden ticket, and I’ve mislaid it. [laughter] We’ll start with whichever is least settled by next week. Good night.

Verdict

Documented:

  • One tally counted 201 visits by both 2024 tickets to the seven swing states; a 2012 USA Today–Gallup poll found 82% of swing-state voters saw a campaign TV ad vs 62% elsewhere (old, search summary). [1]
  • Trump expected about $27.5 million from three California fundraisers and one in Las Vegas; Harris held sold-out Bay Area fundraisers; a political scientist calls fundraising stops a common part of every race in California. [2]
  • Cook’s Oct 23 ratings (as recorded in our own House file) had 186 Solid D and 168 Solid R seats of 434 rated; our Senate list had 15 of 35 races as safe (seven R, eight D). [3]
  • Prop 50 redrew five California districts, and four incumbents are running in a different district; District 41 was Calvert’s for more than 30 years. [4]
  • Nineteen jurisdictions have joined the popular-vote compact (222 electoral votes); it needs 270. [5]

Disputed:

  • Whether the Electoral College is why candidates promise safe states less. The logic (a state that can’t flip has no leverage) is plausible, but no study of promises by state was found. Research on whether swing states get more federal spending is mixed and is a different question. [3]
  • Whether safe states are ignored by both sides in the same way. Shelby’s claim that red voters in blue states and blue voters in red states are equally ignored is consistent with the visit data, but untested.

Unsupported:

  • That representation or constituent service gets worse when a map moves. The plausible mechanism was explained; no study or count was found, and no figure on how many Californians were moved. [4]
  • That fundraisers replaced California campaign stops in 2024 specifically. The search found no state-by-state visit count. [2]
