The One Big Beautiful Bill Act: how it fits my positions

November 7, 2026

Published 2026-11-07. This is the opinion companion to the neutral explainer. The facts about the bill come from the enacted text and are cited there by section number. The quotes in “My position” are from my own political views page. The “fit” labels were drafted with an AI assistant (Claude) and were confirmed by me; where my views page is silent, I say so.

The short version

I started this project with a view: I hate the bill. It gave a small, temporary tax break to most people and a large, permanent one to the wealthy; it cuts Medicaid and food assistance; and, I had heard, it breaks the school system. Checking the text, part of that held up and part did not. The tax and safety-net parts held up. The public-lands part is narrower than I had heard (there is no land sale in the final law, though leasing is mandated), and the schools part is a tax credit and a 529 expansion, not a transfer out of public school budgets.

Taxes

My position. “Taxes should be ‘equitably painful’ — the burden should feel equivalent across income levels.” “Progressive taxation, wealth taxes, reduction or elimination of tax breaks for the wealthy or windfall income, and simplification of the tax system are needed.”

The bill. The 2017 cuts become permanent (Secs. 70101–70107); CBO’s average for 2026–2034 is a loss of about 3.1% for the lowest income decile and a gain of about 2.7% for the top decile. The new deductions for tips, overtime, car-loan interest and seniors end after 2028. The bill adds phase-outs, elections and reporting forms, and the explainer lists several drafting errors.

Fit. Mismatch on fairness and on simplification. By my own “equitably painful” test it fails: the lowest decile loses while the top gains.

Tips. My comment, 2026-10-26: the “no tax on tips” deduction is “a do-nothing noisemaker scam, that may be used by workers who aren’t traditionally tipped. Besides - why should tips not be taxed, if they exist at all??” What the text says (Sec. 70201): a deduction of up to $25,000, not an exclusion; phased out above $150,000 of income ($300,000 joint); limited to occupations that customarily received tips on or before December 31, 2024, on a Treasury list that was not yet written. The occupation date narrows my worry about new “tipped” jobs but does not close it. Earlier I wrote that tip cuts “only help some low-wage workers” and that “all low-wage workers need tax burden reduction, not just tipped workers.”

Estate tax and business expensing. The estate exemption becomes $15 million and permanent; 100% expensing and immediate R&D deduction become permanent. Both are mismatches with “reduction or elimination of tax breaks for the wealthy.”

Health care and the safety net

My position. “Universal healthcare is more cost-effective and produces better outcomes than the profit-based system we have now.” “Everyone should get the same level of health care: citizens, immigrants, visitors, documented or not.” Safety-net programs “should not be tied to a person’s employment.” “Healthcare should get more funding than the military.”

The bill. Medicaid work requirement for expansion adults 19–64 (Sec. 71119); six-month eligibility rechecks (71107); limits on provider taxes and state-directed payments (71115–71116); immigrant eligibility limits for Medicaid, Medicare and ACA credits; SNAP work rules and state cost-sharing starting 2027–2028 (Secs. 10101–10108).

Fit. Strong mismatch. The work requirement is the clearest conflict in the whole bill with my “not tied to employment” position. I have not traced how the Medicaid changes affect disability services; that is an open question, not a finding.

Education

My position. “I don’t support public funding of private schools.” “I don’t support charter schools.” “Education should have more support than the military.” As the parent of a child with a disability, I have appreciated the school system’s support.

The bill. A federal tax credit of up to $1,700 for gifts to scholarship granting organizations, starting 2027, in states that opt in (Sec. 70411). 529 accounts may pay for K-12 tuition, curriculum and tutoring, including at religious schools, with a $20,000 annual cap (Sec. 70413). Nothing in either section sets curriculum or accreditation conditions. The Department of Education is not abolished by the bill. Loan caps, the end of Grad PLUS and a new repayment plan are in Title VIII.

Fit. Mismatch. A tax credit is not a direct grant, so one can argue whether it is “public funding”; I count tax breaks that subsidize private school costs. The “money taken out of public schools” mechanism I had heard about is not how the text works.

Public lands, energy and climate

My position. “I strongly oppose the sale of federal lands for private use.” “I’m skeptical of BLM uses of land for mining and grazing that damage public resources.” “Public lands belong to all Americans and future generations, not private profit.” “I’m also a fan of the Green New Deal.” “We have passed the climate change ‘tipping point’ — and that mainly means we should redouble our efforts to reduce human effects.”

The bill. No federal land sale. It does mandate quarterly onshore oil and gas lease sales (Sec. 50101), caps the coal royalty at 7% through 2034 (50202), sets timber targets (50301), ends most clean-energy credits (70501–70513), and rescinds unspent funds for environment and climate programs. I also checked a podcast’s claims: “200 million acres opened” and several others did not verify against the text.

Fit. Match by omission on land sales; mismatch on leasing, coal, timber and the repeal of clean-energy credits. One tension I have not resolved: the bill funds $1 billion for western water storage (Sec. 50501), and I have written “We need to tear down a lot of dams.”

Immigration, defense and privacy

My position. I “support reductions in trade and immigration restrictions,” with “special emphasis… on refugees and asylum seekers.” “I am strongly opposed to nuclear weapons.” I oppose “government surveillance, especially… face recognition.” “Foreign aid is far less expensive and far more effective than warfare.”

The bill. About $178 billion for border, detention and enforcement; new fees with no waivers for asylum applications, work permits and temporary protected status; a 1% tax on cash remittance transfers; funding for the B-21, Sentinel and a sea-launched nuclear cruise missile; DNA and biometric collection (from the section summaries; not yet verified against the text).

Fit. Strong mismatch on immigration and the remittance tax, and on nuclear forces. The surveillance provisions need a closer read before I make a firm claim.

Where I agree, and where my own views conflict

  • Agreement: the air traffic control modernization, about $12.5 billion (Sec. 40003), fits “modernizing the support systems for our air travel system — not gutting them.”
  • Direct File: I wrote “I do not support government preparation of taxes under our current taxation system,” for privacy reasons. The bill only orders a report on replacing Direct File with private free filing (Sec. 70607). My reason is privacy, not a preference for private software.
  • Spending versus borrowing: I want more spending on health and education, and I dislike borrowing. The bill raises the debt limit by $5 trillion (Sec. 72001).
  • Tax simplicity versus targeted relief: I want a simpler tax code, and I also want targeted help for people who need it. The bill’s relief is targeted, and the result is complicated.
  • Silent: farm programs, spectrum auctions, the SALT cap itself, Trump accounts and HSAs. My views page does not address them.
