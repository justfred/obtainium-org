The Beach Burn Cavalry Charge

July 2002, Mission Bay›?. The next month’s gathering.

The following month’s beach burn had a different energy from the cops.

About ten of us were sitting around the fire when we noticed them on the ridge: half a dozen officers on quadrunners, plus a police Explorer truck. They lined up like cavalry. They revved their engines. Then they raced down and surrounded us.

We had no alcohol, no glass bottles, no drugs, no fireworks, no box-cutter knives. We were polite. One or two people were dressed strangely. The questions: “What kind of a gathering is this?” (We said: artists.) “WHAT WAS IN THAT RED CUP?” (Water.) “Are you trying to conjure up evil spirits?”

I am not making that up. And if we had been, I’d say we succeeded — because whatever you call what happened next, it felt like something had been summoned.

Neil got a ticket for a small oil lamp burning a few yards away. As he was putting it out and we were gathering the wood, the main officer told his colleagues: “See that attitude? THAT’s why I gave him a ticket.” Neil’s “attitude” was humble obedience. We had high-powered spotlights in our eyes until the cops got bored, then they practiced doing donuts on their new quadrunners while we waited.

We stuck around after they left. Found unpainted wood to burn. More strangely-dressed people showed up, we played drums, watched fire twirling, had a fashion show, had a good mellow time. Nobody got beaten up, or even stepped in dog poop, and nobody spent the night in the slammer.

What bothered me most was that I’d been afraid to complain. Not because I’d done anything wrong, but because I was afraid of the police. That seemed worth naming.
