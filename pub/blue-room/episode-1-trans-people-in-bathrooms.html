The Blue Room — Episode 1: Trans People in Bathrooms

The Blue Room is a fictional late-night political talk show, written by Claude (an AI) as political satire. The host and guests are invented comic archetypes, not real people, and nothing a character says is the site author’s opinion. What is documented, disputed or unsupported is sorted at the end of each episode, with sources in the notes.

Last serviced Sunday, November 1, 2026

Wonka Welcome to the Blue Room. Before we start, my guests. [applause]

  • Walt Dunning is a retired actuary and a lifelong copy editor, which means he has priced every risk in this building, including me, and has questions about my spelling. [laughter]
  • Dale Truitt spent twenty years on overnight radio, never entirely believed the official explanation, and never entirely disbelieved it; he is usually right about what happened and cheerfully wrong about why.
  • Margo Bell can tell you tomorrow’s headline tonight, and which network will run it, and will occasionally write it herself. [laughter]
  • Shelby Spinner, a proud MAGA mama bear, whom I’m told has never once been cut off. She would like that on the record. She will be speaking first. And, I suspect, second. [laughter]

Wonka Tonight, an easy one. [laughter] I say that every time, and I have never once been right. Tonight’s subject: who may use which restroom. A few slides first.

1. The map. 21 states plus Puerto Rico limit bathroom use by gender identity. Four have criminal penalties. [1]

2. The poll. 49% favor sex-at-birth rules, 26% oppose. [2]

3. The research. One Massachusetts study found no change in incidents. A national survey finds trans people report harassment. [4][5]

4. The courts. No Supreme Court ruling on bathrooms. Idaho’s law is partly blocked and on appeal. [6]

5. The case both sides cite. Loudoun County, Virginia, 2021. [3]

Shelby Wonka, as a mother of daughters, I’d just like to say that women and girls deserve private, safe spaces, and—

Walt Safe from what, specifically?

Shelby —the American people overwhelmingly understand that men are men and women are women, and a woman should not have to share a restroom with a man. It’s very simple.

Wonka Forty-nine percent, Shelby. A plurality.

Shelby A plurality is a very strong word. [laughter]

Walt A plurality is “more than anyone else and fewer than half,” which is also my bowling average. [laughter]

Margo Okay. So. Here’s how that number gets made. In February 2025 Pew asked 5,097 adults whether trans people should use the bathroom that matches the sex on their birth certificate. Forty-nine percent said yes. Twenty-six said no. And the rest said neither, which is a quarter of the country, and here’s the thing: nobody on this panel is speaking for them. [a long pause]

Wonka Margo, you’ve been waiting all night to do that. [laughter]

Margo The story here is a wedge. It’s a manufactured controversy, and this fall there are ballot measures in Colorado, Arizona, Nebraska and Washington—

Wonka Margo. “Manufactured” is a claim about motive. Nearly half the country holds Shelby’s view, and I’d guess most of them mean it.

Margo Fair. The campaigns are strategic. The voters may be sincere. [a few groans from the left side of the studio] I’d add that “bathroom” is a very efficient word. It does in one syllable what a policy paper takes forty pages to do. That’s why it’s on the ballot.

Wonka New rule: if your campaign slogan fits in one syllable, someone owes the voter a footnote. [laughter] And that goes for both coasts.

Dale In twenty years of overnight radio, the caller who asked “who checks?” was never wrong that nobody was checking. I also once took a call from a man who was certain the bathrooms were bugged by the Pentagon. I let him finish. You always let them finish. Something he said about the pipes has stayed with me. [laughter]

Dale Let me tell you why people don’t trust the institutions. Loudoun County, May 2021: a girl is assaulted in a school restroom, the district moves the boy to another school, and in October it happens again. A grand jury found the superintendent made a false statement at a board meeting. That isn’t a theory. It’s a record.

Wonka It is a record. The assault was real, the handling was bad, and the grand jury said so. Shelby?

Shelby And that’s exactly why we need these laws. These laws would have prevented—

Walt Did the boy use that restroom under a gender-identity policy?

Shelby —they would have prevented that very situation, and Walt, I’ll tell you something else. Privacy isn’t a statistic. A girl shouldn’t have to wait for an incident report before her dignity counts.

Wonka Yes, and. That’s a real point. Privacy is a value, not a number, and a rule can be about comfort without incident data. The argument is whether this rule delivers it, and at what cost. On Loudoun, the record shows an assault and a bad response. It doesn’t show that a policy caused it.

Shelby I’ll give you that much, Wonka. I can’t prove the policy caused it. I just can’t stop thinking about the girl.

Wonka That’s honest, and I’d put it in the verdict if verdicts had room for feelings.

Walt The 2018 Massachusetts study found no change in restroom incidents after protections passed. It’s one state, it counts police reports, which undercount, and its authors support such laws.

Margo And the 2022 survey: trans men using women’s restrooms reported about 10% denied access and 11% harassed in a year. Trans women in men’s rooms, 7% and 9%.

Walt Volunteers, not a random sample.

Margo Sure, Walt, but there’s nothing comparable on the other side.

Walt I price risk for a living, or did. Frequency, severity, who pays. Here the frequency is unknown, the severity is terrible when it happens, and the cost of the rule lands on everyone who walks through a door. That’s why I keep asking for numbers. It’s not a position. It’s an addiction. [laughter]

Dale There’s one number nobody has: how many cis women get challenged for looking “masculine” under these laws. It’s in commentary and a few stories. I can’t give you a figure, and neither can anyone.

Wonka The first honest sentence tonight about a number nobody has. [laughter] The Supreme Court’s June sports ruling gets quoted a lot, but that was sports, and one education group says it doesn’t reach bathrooms.

Shelby The courts will get it right.

Walt Which courts?

Shelby The ones that agree with me. [laughter]

Wonka And twenty-two states have laws like these—

Walt Twenty-one, plus Puerto Rico.

Wonka I was rounding up. It’s a character flaw. Idaho’s law, which reaches many public buildings, was partly blocked June 16 and is on appeal. And enforcement: Texas’s is complaint-driven, with fines on institutions up to $125,000 a day. Supporters say nobody is inspecting anyone. Opponents worry about exactly that. The statutes don’t say how a rule about sex gets applied to a person in a doorway.

Walt Which is my objection to both sides. A rule nobody has measured, and a remedy nobody has defined. What would count as the rule working?

Dale A rule that runs on a stranger’s complaint gets used on strangers. I’m not saying that’s the plan. I’m saying nobody wrote down who decides.

Margo Then here’s my chyron: a problem with no data, and a remedy with no method. [laughter]

Wonka In my experience, whoever decides who decides is a man named Gerald with a clipboard.

Dale Gerald is on my string. [laughter]

Verdict

Documented:

  • 21 states plus Puerto Rico limit bathroom use; four with criminal penalties. [1]
  • Pew: 49% favor, 26% oppose (Feb 2025). [2]
  • The Loudoun assaults happened; a grand jury found the superintendent made a false statement. [3]
  • Trans people report high rates of denial and harassment in restrooms (volunteer survey, 2022). [4]
  • Massachusetts police-record study: no incident increase (one state, advocacy-adjacent, undercounts). [5]
  • Idaho’s law partly blocked June 16, on appeal. The Supreme Court’s June ruling was about sports. [6]

Disputed:

  • Whether these laws make anyone safer (no incident data either way).
  • Whether they are a pretext for discrimination (no uniform motive shown; Pew makes “just an excuse” hard to claim for everyone).
  • How far the sports ruling reaches (advocacy sources split). [6]

Unsupported:

  • That any law or backer requires inspecting anyone’s body.
  • That the Loudoun policy caused the Loudoun assaults.

Audience reactions
